Best time to visit the East Cape, and how to get here

The East Cape has a genuine high season and a genuine low season, and the gap between them is wide. From November through May the weather is dry, the humidity is low, and the Sea of Cortez spends long stretches flat enough to swim off the beach at breakfast without thinking about it. From July through September the same coastline is hot, humid and quiet, with bathwater seas and a real chance of tropical weather.

Neither window is wrong, but they are not interchangeable. This guide covers the season-by-season picture, what each season does to the water, and the practical side of reaching Lighthouse Point Estates: the airport, the drive, the rental car, the grocery stop, and the things that are easier to sort out before you arrive than after.

The short answer: November to May

If you want one window, take November through May. This is the dry half of the year on the East Cape. Rain is rare, humidity is low, and January daytime highs run around 72°F, warm in the sun and cool enough at night to sleep with the doors open. Seas are generally calm, which matters here because almost everything worth doing on this coast happens on or in the water.

It also lines up with the region's headline events. Grey and humpback whales move through the Sea of Cortez in winter, sportfishing runs strong through spring, and Cabo Pulmo dive boats work their most reliable schedule. December through March is the busiest stretch, so book early for the holidays or spring break weeks.

Season by season

Baja seasons do not map neatly onto the four you may be used to. Here is how the year actually behaves on this stretch of coast.

  • November to February: Dry and clear, with January highs around 72°F, cool evenings and very little rain. Peak whale months and peak visitor months. Bring a layer for the terrace after sunset.
  • March to May: Days warm steadily, the water climbs out of its winter low, and crowds thin after spring break. Wind builds through the afternoons, so plan boat trips for the morning.
  • June to August: Hot and increasingly humid. Sea temperatures climb toward 84°F by late summer, which makes for long, easy snorkelling, but midday on land is genuinely hot and shade organises the day.
  • September to October: The shoulder, and the gamble. September is the wettest month and the quietest for visitors. The water is at its warmest and clearest and rates are lowest, but tropical weather is a live possibility. October usually settles down.
  • Hurricane season: Officially May 15 to November 30, with risk concentrated from late August to early October. Most seasons pass without a direct hit here, but storms do reach this coast. Travel insurance is worth the line item inside this window.

What the seasons do to the water

The Sea of Cortez changes character across the year. In midwinter the water is cool enough that a shorty or full wetsuit makes a long snorkel more comfortable, particularly on morning dives at Cabo Pulmo. Visibility is usually good, the surface is often glassy at first light, and the big action is in whales rather than reef fish.

Through spring the water warms week by week. By June it is swimmable without a thought, and by late summer it reaches roughly 84°F, warm enough to float over a reef for an hour without feeling cold. Late summer and early autumn also bring the year's best visibility, which is why experienced divers book the shoulder months everyone else avoids.

The trade-off is wind and swell. Winter northerlies can chop the water up for a day or two, and spring afternoons often blow. Summer is calmer on average but carries the storm risk. Whatever month you choose, the rule holds: get on the water early and let the afternoon do what it wants.

Flying in and the drive

You fly into Los Cabos International Airport (SJD), the region's main gateway. From the terminal to Lighthouse Point Estates is about 60 minutes on paved highway, running north and east through the desert and along the coast toward La Ribera.

It is an easy drive and a scenic one, but arrive in daylight if you can. Livestock on the road, unlit stretches and unfamiliar turns are all harder to manage after dark. If your flight lands late, plan the grocery stop for the next morning.

Conditions on the final approach into the community, and the vehicle we recommend for it, are covered in the pre-arrival notes we send every guest before travel. Read them before you book your rental rather than after.

Renting a car, and what kind

A rental car is effectively necessary on the East Cape. There is no meaningful public transport, taxis are limited and expensive over these distances, and the things you came for are spread along the coast. Almost everyone picks up a car at the airport.

Consider a 4x4 or high-clearance vehicle rather than the cheapest compact. The main highways are paved, but the East Cape's best side trips run down graded dirt roads that corrugate over time and can wash out after summer rain. Some ground clearance turns those roads from a calculation into a non-event. Budget for Mexican liability insurance, which is mandatory and quoted separately.

Groceries, cash and connectivity

Do your main shop on the way out. San José del Cabo has large supermarkets that sit directly on your route from the airport. Buy for the week there: produce, meat and fish, wine and beer, coffee, anything specific your household will not compromise on.

La Ribera, about 20 minutes from the villas, handles the top-ups. The town has smaller grocery stores, a handful of restaurants and a gas station, which covers milk, tortillas, fresh fish and fuel, but not a full week's provisioning for a group.

Carry pesos. Many East Cape services run on cash, including Cabo Pulmo dive shops, panga captains, small local restaurants and most tips. Draw more than you think you need before you leave San José del Cabo, because ATMs out here are scarce and not always working. On connectivity, set expectations low: cell coverage across the East Cape is intermittent and drops out entirely in places, and many homes run Starlink for exactly that reason. Download your maps and anything you need for work before you leave the airport.

How long to stay

Seven nights is the number that works. The drive from SJD and the first day of settling in absorb most of your arrival day, and the East Cape rewards unhurried days more than packed ones. A week gives you a Cabo Pulmo morning, a fishing day, a run into San José del Cabo, an afternoon in La Ribera and still leaves three or four days where the only plan is the beach in front of you.

Ten to fourteen nights suits groups travelling a long way, families with young children, or anyone booking both Casa Suerte North and Casa Suerte South together for a multi-household trip. Four or five nights works if you are coming from the West Coast and know what you want. Shorter and too much of the trip goes to transit.
