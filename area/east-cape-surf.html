East Cape surf: a day-trip guide to the breaks

The East Cape is a surf coast for people who do not mind driving to get there. Between Los Barriles and the corner at Cabo Pulmo, a string of rocky points sits at the end of dirt roads, with no car park, no lifeguard and no cafe. On the right summer swell those points produce long, workable right-handers. On the wrong day, open sand and a warm swim.

That trade-off is the character of surfing here. You go early, accept that some mornings will not deliver, and plan the trip as a small expedition rather than a beach visit. The reward is that even the well-known names rarely carry the crowds you would find at a comparable point in mainland Mexico. Staying at the villas puts you in the middle of the run, close enough to check a spot at first light and be back at the pool before the day heats up.

When the swell actually runs

The East Cape faces broadly east and southeast, so it needs south and southeast swell to work. Those swells arrive between July and September, generated by storm activity in the eastern Pacific and the tropics, and that window is the season. It is when the points break with any consistency, when the water is warm enough for trunks, and when the coast draws travelling surfers.

Be honest about the rest of the year. From roughly November through April the dominant energy comes from the north, the points are largely shadowed from it, and the surf is small and inconsistent. You may find something rideable on a longboard, but planning a winter trip around surf alone is a poor bet. Bring a board for opportunism, not as the reason you came.

Summer is also hurricane season. Distant storms produce the best swell of the year, but a system tracking close brings heavy rain, flooded arroyos and closed roads. Watch the forecast for the week, not just the swell chart.

The breaks, in general terms

Four names come up repeatedly. All four are point setups working off rock and cobble rather than beach sand, all break right, and all want summer south swell. Conditions vary with tide, size and wind, so treat the notes below as orientation rather than a promise about any particular morning.

  • Shipwrecks: The best known of the group and usually the busiest. A right-hand point over a rocky bottom that can run a long way on a solid south swell. It rewards surfers who can already read a point.
  • Nine Palms: A gentler, more forgiving right that breaks over a cobble point. Often the pick for intermediates and longboarders, and the spot most likely to hold a small crowd of travelling surfers in August.
  • La Fortuna: Further out and quieter, another right-hand point that needs a decent swell to switch on. Worth the extra driving on a bigger day, less so on a marginal one.
  • Punta Perfecta: The most remote of the four, out past the Cabo Pulmo corner on rough track. A committing drive for a spot that only works on the right swell, best suited to confident surfers travelling with a full tank and a plan.

The drive is the hard part

Paved road runs along the East Cape corridor, but every break sits at the end of a dirt track. Expect washboard corrugation, sand traps in places, and arroyo crossings that can be soft or washed out after rain. A 4x4 or a genuinely high-clearance vehicle is the right tool. Standard sedans reach some access points in dry conditions, but you will drive slowly and a wet week can strand you.

Allow more time than the distance suggests. Twenty minutes covers the nearest access from the villas; the further points take an hour or more each way off tarmac. Nothing at the breaks is serviced: no fuel, no water, no food and no reliable phone signal. Fill the tank in La Ribera or Los Barriles before you turn off, and tell someone at the house where you are heading and when to expect you back.

What to load in the truck

Pack as though you will be self-sufficient for the whole morning, because you will be. The list below is the minimum.

  • Water: More than you think. Summer heat and a long paddle dehydrate you fast, with nowhere to refill.
  • Shade: A pop-up shelter, or at least a tarp and poles. The points are treeless and the midday sun is punishing.
  • Spare tyre and tools: Check the spare is inflated first. Sidewall cuts on rock are the classic East Cape puncture.
  • Spare fuel: A jerrycan is cheap insurance on the longer runs, especially past Cabo Pulmo.
  • Food: Nothing is open at the beach and the nearest taco stand may be an hour of dirt away.
  • Cash in pesos: For fuel and food on the way through. Card acceptance thins out off the highway.
  • Reef booties and a first-aid kit: Rocky entries and exits make cut feet the most common injury here.

Boards, rentals and lessons

For a summer trip, most surfers want a shortboard for the bigger days and something with more volume for the many mornings that come in waist to chest high. A mid-length or longboard earns its place at the softer points. If you fly in with one board, bring the one that surfs small waves well.

Board rental and lessons are available in the region, mainly in the towns along the corridor and the Los Cabos area rather than at the breaks. Stock is limited and seasonal, so arrange rental in advance and collect the board in town before you drive out. Beginners should learn on sheltered sand, not a rock point.

Etiquette at a local point

These are working local waves with small, established crews, and the ordinary rules matter more here than at a beach break: the surfer closest to the peak has priority, do not drop in, do not paddle around people to take the inside, and wait your turn where the lineup stacks. Sit at the edge of the pack until you have watched the rotation for a few sets.

Beyond that, be an unremarkable visitor. Say hello, keep the group small, park where you are not blocking access, take every scrap of rubbish home, and do not post directions to spots that are quiet for a reason. A little Spanish and a lot of patience go a long way here.

Hazards and safety

These are remote breaks with no lifeguards, no patrol and no quick help. Cell coverage is patchy at most access points, so assume you cannot call anyone, and the nearest reliable medical care is well over an hour away, much of it on dirt.

The risks are straightforward. Rock entries cause cuts, so wear booties and find the channel before you paddle out. Rip currents run along the points and are the main way surfers get into trouble. The summer sun burns through an overcast sky, and dehydration creeps up during a long session. Surf with at least one other person, watch the tide and wind, and be willing to drive home without surfing. The most useful skill here is calling it off.
